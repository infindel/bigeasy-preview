How Do We Clear Northshore Acreage?

Northshore properties run bigger than a city lot, and that changes how a brush clearing job gets planned. A half-acre yard in Mandeville might just need the fence line and a few overgrown beds cut back. A few acres outside Covington or Slidell can mean privet, vines, and volunteer saplings holding thirty or forty feet deep along the tree line, with a gravel driveway or a single gate as the only way equipment gets in. We walk the property first, figure out what access the land actually allows, and bring machines sized to the job instead of showing up with one setup for every property. On tracts headed for forestry mulching or a bigger land-clearing project, brush clearing is usually step one: get the undergrowth down and out of the way before the heavier work starts.

Fence lines take the worst of it out here. Privet, blackberry, and vine grow into and through wire and wood fencing until the fence line disappears and the property boundary is more guess than fact. We cut brush back off the fence itself, not just around it, so the line is visible and the fence stays standing instead of getting pulled apart by what's growing on it. The same goes for property edges next to a neighbor's tree line or a drainage ditch, where brush builds up year after year if nobody touches it.

Regrowth is the honest part of this job nobody skips: Louisiana's wet season and long growing year mean cut brush does not stay cut forever. Vines and fast-growing volunteers will come back along a fence line or a cleared edge, usually faster than most people expect. Clearing it now buys time, opens up the ground, and makes the next round of maintenance a mowing job instead of a machete job. For overgrown rural acreage, a driveway swallowed by brush, or a fence line that has not been visible in years, that is what the crew is there to fix: usable land back, on a property that's built to grow wild again if it's left alone.
