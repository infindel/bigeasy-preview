Do You Need a Permit to Clear Land in New Orleans?

Quick answer

Most straightforward land clearing in New Orleans, brush, undergrowth, small trees, doesn't call for a separate parish permit on its own. Paperwork usually enters the picture once the scope includes demolishing a structure, cutting a protected tree, working a parcel near a wetland, or changing drainage. We aren't attorneys and we don't pull permits for you. We tell you what we see on the walk and point you to your parish to confirm before anything starts.

Every land clearing call in New Orleans eventually lands on the same question: do I need a permit for this. The honest answer is that it depends on what's actually on the parcel and what's being done to it, not on "land clearing" as a blanket category. Cutting back brush on a dry residential lot is a different animal than tearing down a camp near a canal, and requirements can differ between Orleans Parish, Jefferson Parish, St. Tammany, and everywhere in between. Below is how we think through it on a walk, and what we tell owners before a machine ever shows up.

Do You Need a Permit for Routine Land Clearing?

For a lot of jobs, no. Clearing brush, cutting back overgrowth, grinding stumps, and general site prep on a standard residential or commercial parcel is often work a parish doesn't require separate paperwork for. That covers a good share of the calls we get, and it's why "clearing" and "permit job" aren't automatically the same thing in most people's minds. It stops being the baseline the moment the scope changes, which is exactly where owners get caught off guard partway through.

What Can Turn a Clearing Job Into a Permit Job?

A handful of conditions tend to shift a project from routine to regulated. We're watching for these on every walk we do:

  • A structure coming down alongside the land, a house, camp, shed, or outbuilding
  • Trees a parish or city ordinance protects because of species or size
  • A parcel that sits near a wetland, canal, or other protected water feature
  • Work that changes how water moves across or off the property, grading or drainage included

Any one of these is reason enough to make a call to your parish before work starts. Stack two or three together, and it's worth treating the whole project as a permit job from day one rather than finding out midway through. None of this means the work stalls. It usually means the paperwork runs in parallel with the parts of the job that don't need it, so a mulching or brush-clearing phase can often start while a demolition or wetland question gets sorted out separately.

What Does Demolition Add to the Picture?

Taking down a structure is its own animal from clearing the ground around it. A demolition permit is common when a building comes down, and there can be additional steps tied to utility disconnects depending on what's tied into the site. If your project includes tearing down anything with a roof on it, whether that's a house, a garage, or a storm-damaged outbuilding, tell us during the walk. We'll scope the demolition separately from the clearing and flag it as something to run past your parish first, rather than folding it quietly into the rest of the job.

What About Protected Trees and Wetland-Adjacent Land?

Tree protection rules and wetland review vary by where the parcel sits, and they're set by your parish, the city, or in some cases state and federal offices, not by us. If a mature tree looks like it could be a species or size a local ordinance cares about, we say so on the walk instead of guessing on your behalf. The same goes for a lot near standing water, a canal, or a drainage feature. We've written more on how that changes a project in our guide to clearing land near wetlands in Louisiana, and it's worth a read before you get a quote if that description sounds like your property.

Who Do You Actually Call to Confirm?

Your parish is the answer, not us. We're a land clearing and site work contractor, not attorneys, and we do not pull permits on a client's behalf. What we do is walk the property, tell you plainly what we see that could call for paperwork, and make sure you have that in hand before you sign off on scope. If your project is straightforward acreage clearing with none of the flags above, our crew can usually move fast once you're ready. Read more about how a typical project runs in our land clearing in New Orleans guide, or check what a scoped-out tree removal job looks like if a specific tree is the sticking point on your property.

One more thing worth saying plainly: we'd rather lose a day to a phone call with your parish office than start cutting on a parcel that turns out to need a sign-off first. That's not us being cautious for the sake of it. It's the difference between a job that finishes clean and one that stalls halfway with equipment sitting idle while paperwork catches up. Bring us into the conversation early, tell us anything you already know about the property, structures, tree cover, proximity to water, and the estimate we hand you will reflect the real scope instead of a guess.
