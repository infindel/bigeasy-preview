What to Do With a Storm-Damaged Tree in New Orleans

Quick answer

Check the trunk and the base before anything else. A new lean, a split or cracked trunk, or cracked soil lifting around the roots means the tree's structure is compromised and it's a call-a-pro situation, not a wait-and-see one. A single cracked limb on an otherwise healthy tree is usually a smaller job. When in doubt, get a licensed crew to look before the next system moves through.

Southeast Louisiana gets wind and rain that trees don't always walk away from clean. Some damage is obvious. Some sits quiet for weeks and then lets go on an ordinary afternoon. Here's how to tell the difference and what to actually do about it.

What Should You Actually Look For After a Storm?

Walk the property with a specific checklist in mind rather than a general impression. A tree can look fine from the porch and still have a problem at ground level. Start low and work up: the base of the trunk, the trunk itself, then the canopy.

The base is where root-plate damage shows up first. If the ground around the trunk looks disturbed, cracked, or slightly heaved compared to before the storm, the root system has moved even if the tree itself still looks upright. That's often the earliest and easiest sign to miss, because nobody thinks to check the dirt.

Is a Leaning Tree Always an Emergency?

Not always, but any lean that's new is worth taking seriously. Some trees have grown with a natural bend their whole life and that's just their shape. What matters is change: did this tree stand straighter last month? A fresh lean, especially paired with lifted soil on the opposite side of the trunk, usually means the root plate has partially pulled loose. That tree can hold for a while and then come down with no further warning, which is exactly why it shouldn't sit on a "we'll watch it" plan near a house, driveway, or fence line.

A few things to check when you're deciding how urgent a lean is:

  • Has the lean visibly changed since before the storm, or has it always looked this way?
  • Is the soil around the base cracked, mounded, or pulling away on one side?
  • What's within falling distance: a roof, a car, a power line, a fence, a path people actually use?
  • Are there other trees nearby that could come down with it if it goes?

What About a Cracked or Split Limb?

A broken limb still hanging in the canopy, sometimes called a hangar, is its own kind of hazard because gravity and wind are both still working on it. It can come down days later with no warning, and it can take smaller branches with it on the way. If the crack runs into the trunk itself rather than stopping at the limb, that's a different and more serious problem: a split trunk usually means tree removal is the honest answer rather than a prune-and-hope. If the crack stays contained to one limb and the rest of the tree looks sound, that's often a smaller pruning job.

Why Not Just Wait Until After the Next Storm?

Waiting feels like the cheaper option because nothing has to happen today. In practice it tends to work the other way. A tree with a compromised root plate or a cracked trunk doesn't heal between storms, it just sits under stress until wind or saturated ground finishes the job. When that happens on its own schedule, the damage usually lands on something: a roof, a fence, a car, a neighbor's yard. What would have been a planned removal on a clear day becomes an emergency call during or right after weather, when crews are stretched thin and the job is harder and messier because of what it landed on. Handling it while the tree is still standing and the yard is dry is the version that costs less in every sense, not just money.

When Should You Call a Professional?

Call before you climb, before you cut, and before you decide it can wait. Storm-stressed trees don't fall predictably, and a tree with a compromised root plate can shift under its own weight while someone is standing underneath it with a chainsaw. That's not a job for a ladder and a weekend. A licensed, insured crew with the right rigging can take a tree down in sections, working around the house, the driveway, or whatever else is close by, and can tell you honestly whether a tree needs to come down at all or just needs a limb taken off. That's the whole point of a free estimate: get a straight answer before you spend money or risk anyone's safety guessing. Storm Damage FAQs

How do I know if a storm-damaged tree needs to come down? +
Look at the trunk and the base first. A tree leaning more than it used to, a cracked or split trunk, or soil lifting and cracking around the root plate are all signs the tree's structure is compromised. Any of those, and it's worth a call rather than a guess.
Is it safe to remove a leaning tree myself? +
Generally no. A tree under stress from wind or a shifted root system doesn't fall the way an undamaged tree does, and it can move without warning. That's a job for a licensed, insured crew with the right rigging and equipment, especially near a house, fence, or power line.
Can a damaged tree be saved instead of removed? +
Sometimes. A single broken limb on an otherwise sound tree is often a pruning job, not a removal. The deciding factors are how much of the canopy is affected, whether the trunk itself is cracked, and whether the root plate has lifted. We tell you plainly which category your tree is in during the estimate.
