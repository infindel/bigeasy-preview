How Do We Remove Trees on Tight Lots?

Metairie lots run tight. Fifty and sixty-foot frontages, houses close on both sides, a chain-link fence a few feet from the trunk, and a slab foundation next door nobody wants a falling limb landing on. That changes how a tree comes down. On open acreage you fell the whole tree in one direction and clean up after. On a typical lot between Causeway and Bonnabel, or off Veterans near the canal, there usually isn't a direction that misses something: a shed, a driveway, a neighbor's roofline, an AC unit, overhead lines feeding two houses at once. Our crew reads the lot before a saw ever starts, and looks at what has to stay standing, not just what has to come down.

That usually means taking the tree apart in pieces instead of dropping it whole. Limbs get roped and lowered by hand or lifted clear with rigging, cut back section by section until what's left is a trunk we can drop into the one open lane the property allows. Fences get padded, and flower beds, sprinkler heads, and driveways get covered before the first cut. If the tree sits near a property line, we talk through what the neighbor needs to know before work starts, because a limb swinging into their yard is still their yard. Old live oaks common in established Metairie neighborhoods carry a lot of weight in low, wide limbs that lean over roofs and carports. Storm-cracked pines and water oaks near drainage canals tend to be more urgent, since a root ball that starts lifting after heavy rain is on borrowed time. Whatever the reason, dead, storm-damaged, or simply in the way of a new driveway or renovation, we take it down piece by piece where the lot demands it, grind the stump if you want the yard usable again, and haul every bit of debris off the property. You're not left with logs and brush to deal with on your own. The estimate is free, fast, and accurate, and it accounts for the access problem before we ever start cutting, not after.
